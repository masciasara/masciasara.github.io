## A surprise from JWST

Among the thousands of galaxies JWST has revealed in the early Universe, a particular class keeps appearing: compact, luminous sources with unusually broad emission lines whose nature is still debated. We call them **Little Red Dots** (LRDs) — though the reddest ones are just one end of a wider population. I focus on the bluest among them: the **compact broad-line emitters** (cbBLEs).

They are something JWST is showing us for the first time, and understanding them is one of the central questions I am currently working on.

---

## What they look like

LRDs are unresolved or barely resolved even by JWST in the optical, concentrating enormous luminosity into a tiny volume. The cbBLEs I study are the blue end of this population. Their spectra share several distinctive features:

- **Broad Balmer emission lines** — line widths of hundreds to thousands of km/s, far broader than typical star-forming galaxies
- **Compact morphology** — no extended host galaxy visible, even at JWST resolution
- **High ionization** — strong UV emission lines including He II and N IV], which require extremely hard ionizing spectra
- **Low Balmer decrements** — indicating low dust content and conditions consistent with high LyC escape fractions
- **Blue UV slopes** — consistent with young stellar populations, AGN contribution, or both

---

## The connection to Little Red Dots

The nature of LRDs is still debated — AGN, extremely dense stellar systems, or something in between. My research argues that cbBLEs and LRDs may be **the same population viewed from different angles**.

In this picture, these objects host an AGN surrounded by a polar outflow and an equatorial inflow. Viewed pole-on, the source appears blue and compact — what we identify as a cbBLE. Viewed at larger angles, dust along the equatorial plane reddens the emission — producing what we see as a Little Red Dot. The same object, different line of sight.

This geometry is being modeled using **[Sirocco](https://sirocco-rt.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)**, a Monte Carlo radiative transfer code, which can simulate how radiation propagates through different gas and dust configurations and reproduce the spectral diversity we observe across this population.

## The Balmer break as a window onto LyC escape

One of the key diagnostics I use to connect cbBLEs to reionization is the **Balmer break** — a spectral feature at 3646 Å arising from hydrogen absorption. 

In cbBLEs — the pole-on sources in our unified picture — we observe weaker Balmer breaks compared to the redder LRD population viewed at larger angles. This is consistent with the geometry: looking straight down the outflow channel, we see both bluer emission and evidence for ionizing photon escape. The Balmer break thus becomes a way to connect the viewing angle, the spectral appearance, and the ionizing properties of these sources within a single framework.

## Why they matter for reionization

If cbBLEs are viewed pole-on through a low-density outflow channel, ionizing photons can escape along that same line of sight. This makes them compelling candidates for significant contributors to cosmic reionization — potentially with escape fractions much higher than the typical star-forming galaxy.

Despite being rare, a high escape fraction could give them an outsized role in the ionizing photon budget. Quantifying that contribution — combining spectral modeling, stacking analyses, and comparisons with the broader galaxy population — is the focus of my current work.

This is ongoing research and the paper is in preparation.

## Once upon a time, there was nothing. Then a bang occurred.

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the Universe went through a dramatic change: protons and electrons combined to form neutral hydrogen for the first time, releasing the light we now detect as the Cosmic Microwave Background. The Universe became transparent -- but also dark. For hundreds of millions of years, it remained cold, neutral, and silent. Astronomers call this the **cosmic Dark Ages**.

Then, somewhere between 200 million and one billion years after the Big Bang, the first stars and galaxies ignited. Their ultraviolet radiation — specifically photons energetic enough to ionize hydrogen -- began to carve out bubbles of ionized gas in the surrounding intergalactic medium. These bubbles grew, expanded, and eventually overlapped, transforming the entire IGM from neutral to ionized.

This transformation is called **cosmic reionization**. It is the last major phase transition in the history of the Universe — and we still don't fully understand what caused it.

---

## The central question

Which sources produced enough ionizing photons to reionize the Universe -- and how did those photons escape?

Three ingredients matter: how efficiently galaxies convert their starlight into ionizing radiation; how many galaxies exist and how bright they are; and what fraction of those ionizing photons actually escape into the intergalactic medium rather than being absorbed within the galaxy itself.

Each carries uncertainty. But the biggest unknown -- and the one I focus on -- is **the escape fraction**. An average of around 10% across all star-forming galaxies is required to match the timing of reionization predicted by independent constraints, including the CMB optical depth and quasar absorption spectra.

---

## How we know reionization happened

Several independent observational probes constrain the timeline:

- **The CMB** — free electrons produced during reionization scatter CMB photons, leaving an imprint on the polarization spectrum. Current measurements place the midpoint of reionization at z ~ 7.7 ± 0.7
- **Lyα emission from high-redshift galaxies** — Lyα photons are absorbed by neutral hydrogen, so the fraction of Lyα-emitting galaxies drops sharply above z ~ 6, directly tracing the neutral fraction of the IGM
- **Quasar absorption spectra** — the Gunn-Peterson trough, a near-total suppression of flux in distant quasar spectra at the right wavelengths, marks the endpoint of reionization around z ~ 5.5

Together, these probes paint a picture of **late and patchy reionization** -- inhomogeneous, with ionized bubbles forming first around the densest regions and gradually merging until the process was complete.

## Who drove reionization?

The leading candidates are **young, hot, massive stars** in early star-forming galaxies -- O and B-type stars that emit copious UV radiation. Their host galaxies were far more numerous than AGN in the early Universe, making them the most plausible dominant source.

AGN have also been proposed as contributors. They have high intrinsic escape fractions and hard ionizing spectra, and recent JWST observations have revealed a larger population of faint, high-redshift AGN than previously known -- including the enigmatic **Little Red Dots**. Whether they played a significant role in reionization remains genuinely open. My current research on compact broad-line emitters engages directly with this debate.

In either case, the critical unknown is not photon production -- it is escape. Most ionizing photons are absorbed within their host galaxies before reaching the IGM. Understanding which galaxies let that light out, and why, is the key to understanding reionization.

---

## What the numbers say

My work on the CEERS survey finds that galaxies currently observable with JWST — those above a certain luminosity threshold — contribute **less than 35% of the ionizing budget** needed to sustain reionization. The rest must come from fainter, lower-mass sources we cannot yet fully characterize.

This points to one of the most pressing open questions in the field: what is the escape fraction of the faintest galaxies, at the very bottom of the luminosity function? Gravitational lensing, which magnifies distant background sources, is the best tool we have to begin answering it.

About 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the Universe went dark.

The first atoms had just formed — mostly hydrogen — and there was nothing yet to produce light. For hundreds of millions of years, the cosmos was cold, dark, and silent. Astronomers call this the **cosmic Dark Ages**.

Then, somewhere between 200 million and one billion years after the Big Bang, the first stars and galaxies ignited. Their light was energetic enough to strip electrons from hydrogen atoms — a process called **ionization** — and slowly, patch by patch, the intergalactic medium transformed from a neutral fog into the transparent, ionized plasma we observe today.

This transformation is called **cosmic reionization**. It is one of the major phase transitions in the history of the Universe, and we still don't fully understand what caused it.

---

## The central question

Which sources produced enough ionizing photons to reionize the Universe — and how did those photons escape into the intergalactic medium?

Star-forming galaxies are the leading candidates. But ionizing photons are easily absorbed by gas and dust inside galaxies. For reionization to happen, a significant fraction of that light needs to *escape* — and we don't yet know which galaxies allow this, or why.

---

## How we study it

The epoch of reionization is too distant to observe directly in most wavelengths. Instead, we look for its signatures:

- **Lyman-alpha emission** — a spectral line of hydrogen that gets absorbed by neutral gas, so its presence (or absence) tells us about the ionization state of the surrounding medium
- **The UV luminosity function** — how many galaxies existed at different brightnesses, which tells us how much ionizing light was available
- **Lyman continuum escape fractions** — the fraction of ionizing photons that actually escape a galaxy into the intergalactic medium

JWST has transformed this field, allowing us to observe galaxies in the first billion years of cosmic history with unprecedented detail.
